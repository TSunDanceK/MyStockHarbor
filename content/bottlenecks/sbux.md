summary: >
  Starbucks controls almost all of its own coffee purchasing, roasting, and
  packaging, so its real supply-side dependencies sit one layer down - in
  the trading houses that source its green coffee and a small number of
  exclusive equipment and ingredient partners. On the customer side,
  Starbucks has no single external buyer the way a component supplier does;
  its revenue comes from tens of millions of individual coffee purchases a
  week. But it does have real, named, concentrated licensing and
  distribution partnerships - most notably with Nestlé and PepsiCo - that
  its own 10-K explicitly flags as concentration risks.
customersNote: >
  Starbucks' revenue is overwhelmingly diversified retail - tens of millions
  of individual coffee purchases across roughly 40,000 stores worldwide - so
  there's no company that "buys from Starbucks" the way an automaker buys
  chips from a supplier. Instead, this chart shows Starbucks' genuinely
  concentrated brand-licensing and distribution partnerships: companies
  that pay Starbucks (via royalties, product sales, or joint-venture profit
  share) for rights to manufacture, distribute, or operate Starbucks-branded
  business outside its own company-run stores. Starbucks' own 10-K names
  both its Channel Development business and its retail licensed operations
  as concentration risks.
