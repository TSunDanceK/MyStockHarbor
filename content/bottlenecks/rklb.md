Rocket Lab's dependency profile doesn't look like a typical hardware
company's. Where most launch and satellite businesses list dozens of
component suppliers, Rocket Lab has spent nearly a decade acquiring or
building nearly its entire stack in-house — engines, avionics, composite
airframes, satellite buses, solar cells, and now (via Mynaric and Motiv
Space Systems) laser communications and robotics too. What's left on the
supply side is a short list of specialized manufacturing-equipment makers
and satellite-component subcontractors tied to its most complex current
programs: the Electroimpact fiber-placement machine industrializing Neutron
production, the Nikon SLM Solutions metal-printing platforms extending its
3D-printed engine strategy, and a cluster of RTX-linked and independent
subcontractors (Collins Aerospace, SEAKR Engineering, Redwire Space, Parsons
Corporation) building the radios, antennas, and ground software for its
Space Development Agency satellite constellation.

On the customer side, the concentration is real and disclosed: Rocket Lab's
own SEC filings show a single U.S. government customer accounted for 29% of
revenue through the first nine months of 2025, anchored by national security
launch contracts and Space Force satellite awards like the $816 million
Tranche 3 deal. The rest of the business is a genuinely diversified base of
commercial Earth-observation and SAR-satellite operators — BlackSky, Planet
Labs, HawkEye 360, Capella Space, Synspective, iQPS, Kinéis, OHB Group, and
Canon Electronics — most of them repeat, multi-launch customers rather than
one-off missions.
