## The thesis

Every explanation of the memory shortage comes down to the same sentence: the AI companies bought it all. That is true, and it is not the answer. If demand were the whole story, Samsung, SK hynix and Micron — three of the most capable manufacturers on Earth, sitting on the largest profits in their history — would be flooding this market. They are not doing it. They cannot do it.

The constraint is not where the chips are made. It is where they are stacked. HBM is not a stick of memory, it is a tower: twelve wafer-thin dies pressed onto one another and aligned to fractions of a micron. The machines that do that stacking come from a list of companies short enough to write on a napkin — Hanmi Semiconductor in Seoul, ASMPT in Hong Kong, and BE Semiconductor Industries in the Netherlands. Retail bought the memory makers, and that trade worked. The duller question is who sells the ability to make the memory.

## Key numbers

- **$90 → $459** — what a 32GB DDR5 kit cost twelve months ago versus today. Five times. Gartner now expects the entry-level PC to disappear from shelves altogether, and in June a group of consumers and small repair shops took the three companies that make almost all of the world's memory to federal court in California
- **Supply gets worse through 2028** — Samsung's memory division said so on its July earnings call; Micron's chief executive went further and told investors he has no line of sight to when supply catches demand. Three years of shortage, forecast by the only people on the planet with a commercial reason to promise the opposite
- **~71% global TC bonder share** — Hanmi's own reported figure for the tool stacking memory today. Micron named Hanmi a top supplier last year, and in June SK hynix placed a ₩44.2bn order for the next-generation TC Bonder 4.5 — large enough that Hanmi was legally required to disclose it. Hanmi has no US listing
- **~250 hybrid bonders a year** — Besi's own capacity at full stretch, per CEO Richard Blickman. That is the leader in the next-generation tool, not an industry total. Qualifying one of these machines at a single logic customer took over three years
- **€987.6M TTM orders** — a company record for Besi. Q2-26 orders €292.9M (+128.8% YoY), revenue €249.9M (+68.7%), gross margin 65.7%, and hybrid bonding customers up from 15 at year-end 2025 to 21

## The setup

To feed a modern GPU you have to put the memory almost close enough to touch the processor and then open a firehose between them — 2,048 bits at once, per stack, against 64 for the stick in your desktop. There is not enough edge on a chip to attach that many wires sideways, so the industry went vertical. Grind a memory die down to thirty microns, roughly a third the width of a human hair, drill more than eight thousand copper channels straight down through it, then do that twelve times and stack them. The finished tower has to come in under 775 microns — and that is the *relaxed* limit. JEDEC raised the ceiling from 720 specifically so this generation could exist. The roadmap says sixteen layers.

Each layer is pressed onto the one below it, heated, and aligned to a fraction of a micron, with thousands of copper contacts landing on thousands of copper contacts. Miss on the second-to-last layer and you have not lost a chip — you have lost every perfect layer underneath it. The next-generation tool is stranger still: it throws the solder away and welds bare copper directly to bare copper, which demands placement accuracy of about a hundred nanometres, surfaces polished flatter than any mirror, and no particles at all. A single speck of dust does not create a small defect; it creates a void up to twenty times larger than the speck itself, and the bond fails.

Which is why "just buy more machines" does not work. These tools are built by hand in cleanrooms and come out slowly, and before a chipmaker will run one in production it has to be qualified — proven on their line, their process, their yields, generation by generation. The moat is not a patent and it is not a factory. It is time, and nobody in this industry can afford to spend it twice.

## Risk factors

- **The transition has already slipped once.** The industry spent two years assuming bumpless copper-to-copper bonding would arrive with this generation of HBM. It did not — JEDEC relaxed the height limit, and Samsung and SK hynix each found other ways to pull the heat out of the stack, pushing hybrid bonding to the generation after. It can slip again
- **The market leader says the revolution is years away.** Hanmi, which sells the *current* tool, states publicly that its bonders are good enough for the next two generations of memory and that the switch does not happen in volume until the end of this decade. That is the single largest risk in this thesis, and it sits directly underneath the valuation
- **Besi is priced for a transition that has not fully arrived.** As of April, all three of the world's memory makers have Besi hybrid bonders on their floors being *evaluated* — Besi will not say which is which, and an evaluation is not an order. The order book is real; the memory revenue behind it is still largely ahead
- **The biggest pure-play is the hardest to own.** Hanmi trades only on the KOSPI (042700), with no US listing or ADR. Besi is the accessible name (Euronext: BESI / OTC: BESIY) and ASMPT is Hong Kong-listed (0522.HK / ASMVY) — meaning access, not conviction alone, shapes where a lot of this flow ends up

## What to watch

The first thing is whether hybrid bonding holds its new date or slips again. That single question decides whether Besi's record order book converts into memory revenue in 2027 or closer to 2030, and it is the one variable the valuation cannot absorb. The second is already being poured: Hanmi finishes a new plant in Incheon in the first half of next year, and Besi is expanding too — both building for a transition neither can date, because the only thing anyone agrees on is that when it arrives there will not be enough machines. Besi's Q3-26 numbers in October are the next checkpoint on whether that order book is still compounding.
