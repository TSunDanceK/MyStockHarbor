## The thesis

Not long ago, Unusual Machines sold video goggles to people who race drones as a hobby. It went public in a tiny offering that raised just $5 million, and on its first day of trading the stock closed below the offer price. Today the market values it at more than a billion dollars. Most people put that down to politics, a Trump trade. The video argues that may be only part of the story, and that the missing part may matter more.

Washington's drone strategy runs into a problem: almost nobody in America makes the parts. Small, cheap, expendable drones are built to be used up, so the winner may be whoever can supply motors, cameras and controllers in huge numbers, quickly and at low cost. The question the video asks is whether a company that started with hobby goggles can become that supplier before the money runs out.

## Key numbers

- **$16.7M Q2 2026 revenue** — up 687% year over year, mostly from enterprise and defence customers.
- **$25M internal Q4 target** — management's own target, not a result. The Q3 target is $12M to $14M, a dip while motor capacity is built.
- **~$230M cash, no debt** — at June 30, raised largely through new share sales.
- **~50M shares outstanding** — up about a third from roughly 38M in December 2025.
- **42% of Q2 revenue from one customer** — a concentration that cuts both ways.
- **$12.8M Army motor order** — the US Army's 101st Airborne Division ordered thousands of American-made motors, in a deal the Financial Times reported at that figure. The Army has since said it plans a follow-on order.

## The setup

The company traces back to 2022, when Red Cat agreed to sell its consumer business, the Fat Shark goggles and the Rotor Riot parts store, to a new company run by Red Cat's former chief operating officer, Allan Evans. Hobby pilots crash constantly, so the whole culture runs on cheap, swappable, repairable parts. Then the war in Ukraine made small first-person-view drones one of its defining weapons, and the Pentagon's drone programme began looking to racers and hobbyists. One of its strongest entrants, Neros, was founded by a former drone-racing champion.

Take one of those drones apart and the motor sits at the heart of it, alongside a flight controller, a battery, a camera and a radio link. Unusual Machines makes, or is building the capacity to make, most of that list, and has agreed to buy a battery maker, Upgrade Energy, to own the powertrain from battery to motor. The video frames it as shovels in a gold rush: plenty of firms are racing to build the finished drone, far fewer make the parts every one of them needs.

A rival can copy a motor. What may be scarce is an American-made one, in volume, with the paperwork to prove where every piece came from. That matters because, by one estimate, China makes almost all the world's rare-earth magnets. The company says it offers both a Made-in-USA motor and a globally sourced one. It winds its own motors in Orlando, but says the high-speed line is still being installed and that supply constraints may last into next year. Motors also wear out first when a drone is crashed, which could mean repeat business rather than one-off sales.

On the stock, the shares have responded to more than one thing. They surged when Donald Trump Jr. joined the advisory board weeks after the 2024 election, and again when the Wall Street Journal reported the administration was in talks to fund a handful of drone companies. The company says he played no role in its Pentagon deals, and the video found no confirmed funding deal. The stock also jumped when the FCC moved against foreign drones. At early-October prices the 52-week range ran from about $7 to about $35. Politics may have lit the match; the factory may decide whether it keeps burning.

## Risk factors

- **Revenue dip before the ramp:** management has flagged that revenue may dip this quarter while capacity is built, ahead of a much bigger fourth quarter. Plans can slip, and targets aren't results.
- **Customer concentration:** one customer made up 42% of Q2 revenue, so a pause from that buyer might hit a quarter directly.
- **Quality of earnings:** H1 net income of $2.5M came from investment gains, not parts, and the H1 operating loss was $15.1M. The company is also investing in some of its own customers and partners, which can blur where real demand ends and its own money begins.
- **Dilution:** the share count has climbed by about a third in six months to fund the build-out, and further raises may follow.
- **Governance:** executives sold shares under pre-arranged plans in August, a warrant grant for the chief executive passed by a modest margin (9.21M to 7.63M), and he now also chairs another company the firm has invested in.
- **Valuation and policy:** the valuation leaves little room for mistakes, and a policy tailwind can fade if priorities change.
- **Easy to copy:** cheap parts are easy to copy, and if rivals build their own American motors, margins might tighten before the company has scaled.

## What to watch

The factory is the first test: management's internal target for a much bigger fourth quarter depends on the motor line coming online. Batteries are the second, since the Upgrade Energy deal still has to close and then be integrated. The third is the Pentagon, with the next rounds of the Drone Dominance programme and whether a reported funding deal ever becomes real. The next earnings report, expected in mid-November, may show which way this is heading.

A real strategic gap, a young factory and a share count that keeps growing to fund it: that tension is the story to follow. This is not financial advice.
