## The thesis

Right now, above your head, thousands of satellites are keeping your phone connected, your bank synced, your GPS locked. Almost every single one of them got there on one company's rocket. For eight years, one small company has been trying to become the second option — and for most of those eight years, Wall Street treated it like a punchline. Nobody was laughing at the valuation. They were laughing at the ambition.

That joke stopped being funny this summer. Rocket Lab just made an $8 billion bet that turned it into something even SpaceX hasn't fully built yet: the only other vertically integrated, satellite-owning launch company on the planet.

## Key numbers

- **$200.3M Q1 2026 revenue, +63.5% YoY** — first quarter above $200M; beat the top of its own guidance range; Space Systems at $136.7M now represents nearly two-thirds of total revenue
- **43% non-GAAP gross margin** — up from 33.4% a year ago; nearly 10-point expansion confirms higher-value Space Systems work is meaningfully shifting the mix
- **$2.2B contracted backlog, +108% YoY** — more than doubled in 12 months; 36% expected to convert to revenue within the next 12 months alone; 70+ Electron missions queued
- **$225–240M Q2 2026 guidance** — beat Wall Street estimates; Iridium adds $871.7M in annual revenue at 57% margin once the deal closes mid-2027
- **$2B+ available liquidity** — plus a $3.6B bridge loan from Deutsche Bank and Wells Fargo funding the Iridium acquisition without immediate dilution
- **90+ missions flown** — world's second-most active launch provider behind only SpaceX; 36 new launch contracts signed in Q1 2026 alone — more than all 21 missions flown in full-year 2025

## The setup

Electron changed the rules for small satellites. Before it, getting to orbit meant ridesharing on someone else's rocket, on someone else's schedule, dropped off wherever the primary payload was going — sometimes waiting the better part of a year for a slot. Electron flies when you need it, to the exact orbit you need. That reliability built the order book that is now accelerating faster than Rocket Lab's own production line.

But launch is only part of the story. Rocket Lab builds engines, avionics, composite rocket bodies, satellite buses, and communication arrays — nearly the entire stack, in-house. The company controls the entire lifecycle of a satellite from the first bolt to the final orbit. The Iridium acquisition folds a profitable $871.7M revenue business at 57% margin onto a launch company that, on its own, is still losing money — doing more for the income statement than years of organic growth could achieve. Rocket Lab isn't adding satellites. It's adding an already-working, already-profitable satellite network. That is what most investors describing this as "the cheaper SpaceX alternative" have not yet priced in.

The market is still punishing this stock — more than 45% below its 52-week high of $151 — primarily because Neutron, the larger next-generation rocket, suffered a propellant tank rupture during testing on 21 January, pushing its maiden flight back to no earlier than Q4 2026. That is the second major delay in fifteen months. The company owned the failure publicly, named the specific manufacturing step that caused it, and is shifting tank production from hand-laid to automated fiber placement. It is a real setback, not a footnote — and the market has not fully forgiven it even as the rest of the business compounds underneath.

## Risk factors

- **Neutron execution risk:** Two delays in fifteen months. If Neutron slips again, the market will ask hard questions about execution regardless of how strong the Iridium numbers look. This is the single most important binary event on Rocket Lab's calendar.
- **Iridium integration complexity:** An $8B acquisition mid-ramp adds significant management bandwidth strain. The deal targets a mid-2027 close — integration risk runs for at least 18 months before the $871.7M revenue and 57% margin are fully in the books.
- **Starship long-term risk:** SpaceX is targeting costs as low as $100–$200 per kilogram to orbit once Starship is fully reusable. However, SpaceX has never once reused a Starship upper stage, in-space refuelling has not been attempted, and the newest Starship version only made its debut flight in May 2026. The economics bears are pricing in haven't been demonstrated once — let alone at the routine cadence required to actually undercut Neutron's dedicated, schedule-certain niche.
- **Pre-profitability valuation:** Market cap of ~$50B with P/E N/M — investors are pricing the platform, not current earnings. Any execution miss at Neutron or Iridium integration compresses a multiple with no earnings floor beneath it.

## What to watch

Neutron's maiden flight is the defining catalyst — if it flies clean before the end of 2026, Rocket Lab stops being "the company trying to become the second SpaceX" and becomes the only other vertically integrated, satellite-owning launch company on the planet. If it slips a third time, execution questions dominate regardless of Iridium. Watch the Iridium deal close timeline for mid-2027 — each quarter of progress toward close de-risks the $871.7M revenue addition. Wall Street's average price target sits near $113, roughly 39% above where the stock trades today even after the 16% pop on the Iridium announcement. The gap between that target and the current price is entirely explained by Neutron. One clean flight closes most of it.
